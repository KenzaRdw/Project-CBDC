\begin{abstract}
This paper studies how the discussion of economists and central banks on central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) evolved between 
2014 and 2026, and how it relates to the policy environment. Using 4,710 research papers and central bank texts, we combine 
bibliometric methods with text analysis. We find that the debate grew quickly but remains concentrated: economists from central 
banks and international institutions write 10\% of the papers but receive 48\% of the citations. Over time, CBDCs are seen less 
as a monetary policy instrument and more as a tool for financial inclusion, payments, and geopolitics. Central banks also became 
much more favorable to CBDCs, while research economists remain more cautious. Finally, this evolution partly follows the policy 
environment: attention rises after some major events, but most changes happen gradually.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}\contentstart

Earlier in 2016, central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) were a topic of discussion, with few papers per year. 
Today, many central banks are studying, testing or running one, and the economic literature on them has 
significantly increased. Most of this literature asks what a CBDC would do to monetary policy, bank funding or 
payment systems (Barrdear and Kumhof, 2016; Brunnermeier and Niepelt, 2019; Agur, Ari and Dell’Ariccia, 2019). 
Much less is known about the discussion itself: has it changed along with the projects it describes? This paper 
asks:

\emph{How has economists’ and institutional discourse on CBDCs evolved between 2014 and 2026, and to what extent does 
this evolution align with changes in the policy environment?}

Before going further, three terms in this question need to be defined. “Economists’ and institutional discourse” 
refers to research written by economists, together with central bank speeches and documents of central banks and 
international institutions. Thus, we treat these two groups separately, since their role in the debate is not 
the same. “Evolved” covers four dimensions: who writes, what is written, how CBDCs are discussed (tone and 
stance), and why they are studied or justified. Finally, “align” means whether changes in the discussion 
coincide in time with policy events, such as the ECB’s reports or the launch of national CBDCs, with the 
geopolitical context as well. In fact, we do not claim that these developments cause them.

As an answer we build a corpus of 4,710 texts written by economists in 39 languages: 3,994 research texts, 511 
central bank speeches and 205 institutional documents, selected from 48,188 harvested documents. We combine 
bibliometric tools with text-as-data methods, then we check each automatic measure against our own reading of a 
sample of texts.

We find that the discussion moves away from monetary policy and private cryptocurrencies, towards national 
projects, financial inclusion, cross-border payments and geopolitics. The tone becomes more positive, and the 
shift towards an explicitly favourable stance is much larger among central banks and institutions than among 
research economists, who remain mostly neutral. In addition to that, policy events sometimes raise the number 
of publications, but none of them changes the tone, which evolves gradually. These results are descriptive 
since they document timing, not causes.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on CBDCs and previous bibliometric studies. 
Section 3 presents the data and how we select the texts written by economists. Section 4 studies who writes 
about CBDCs, Section 5 what is written, Section 6 how CBDCs are discussed (tone and stance), and Section 7 why 
they are studied or justified. Section 8 examines when these changes occur relative to the policy environment. 
Section 9 combines and discusses the findings period by period. Section 10 presents the robustness checks and 
finally Section 11 and 12 discusses the results and their limitations and concludes.

\section{Related literature}\label{sec:literature}
\subsection{Central bank digital currencies}
A central bank digital currency (CBDC) is a digital money issued by a central bank that 
can be used as an instrument of payment and store of value. The term CBDC is recent, 
however, the idea of giving the public access to central-bank money in a non-cash form 
is old. In fact, \textcite{tobin1987} suggested the idea of “deposited currency accounts,”
which combine the advantages of bank deposits along with the safety of central-bank money.

The modern CBDC literature arrived much later, especially as digital payments expanded 
and central banks started to consider their own digital currencies. The early contributions 
were mainly focused on the implications of CBDCs for monetary policy, banking and financial 
stability. \textcite{barrdear2016} examined the macroeconomics of issuing an interest-bearing 
CBDC, while \textcite{bordo2017} explored the possibility of using it to conduct monetary policy.

These questions progressively led to a larger literature on how CBDCs should be structured. 
Developing CBDC involves several choices, for instance whether it should bear interest, 
whether it should be account- or token-based, widely accessible, and how privacy should 
be protected. These choices can involve compromises between payment efficiency, financial 
inclusion, as well as the risks of bank disintermediation or financial instability. 
\textcite{agur2019} analyzed these trade-offs, while \textcite{bindseil2020} discusses 
how a tiered payment system could limit large transfers from bank deposits into CBDCs.

The debate has recently moved beyond monetary policy and banking. As central banks began 
experimenting with concrete projects, questions regarding payment efficiency, financial 
inclusion, privacy, cross-border payments and monetary sovereignty became important. 
Therefore, the literature moved from a focus on what a CBDC could do to the monetary 
and banking system to a broader discussion about how it should be designed and what policy 
objectives it should achieve.

Our paper offers a different perspective within this literature. Rather than identifying 
economic effects of CBDCs, we study how the economic and institutional debate around them 
has evolved over the years.
\subsection{What the field builds on}% reading list + co-citation clusters (Section~\ref{sec:who})
The CBDC literature builds on three foundational trade-offs that structure the debate.

The first concerns privacy and state-issued digital money. \textcite{chaum1983} showed how cryptographic techniques could allow 
electronic payments while preserving users’ privacy, at the other hand, \textcite{nakamoto2008} later demonstrated that digital 
payments could operate without a trusted central intermediary. CBDCs bring these questions into different institutional context: 
can a central bank provide digital money while preserving a degree of privacy comparable to that traditionally associated with 
cash?

The second is related to financial efficiency and financial stability. The classic banking literature highlights the role of banks 
in providing liquidity and transforming deposits into credit \parencite{diamond1983}, while \textcite{tobin1987} proposed giving 
the public access to a safer form of central-bank money. Therefore, CBDCs revive this trade-off. As matter of fact, they may 
improve the safety and efficiency of payments, however they could also draw funds away from commercial banks and affect credit 
provision \parencite{andolfatto2020,brunnermeier2019}.

The third deals with monetary sovereignty particularly the digital age. In fact, the development of cryptocurrencies and stablecoins 
has raised many questions about the coexistence of public and private forms of digital money and about the ability of central 
banks to preserve monetary sovereignty. These concerns have progressively pushed the CBDC debate beyond monetary policy and 
payment design including cross-border and geopolitical considerations.

Our paper does not attempt to resolve these tensions. Instead, it studies how their relative importance in economic and 
institutional discourse has evolved between 2014 and 2026.

\subsection{Bibliometric and text-as-data studies of CBDCs}

Within our corpus, we observe 29 titles that announce a literature,
systematic or bibliometric review of CBDCs, published between 2019 and
2026. When comparing the five most recent bibliometric studies 
\parencite{kvedaraviciute2024,fahad2024,bapat2025,prodan2024,paulovici2026} with ours, 
we found three shared main limits. First, their
data come from one or two databases, Scopus and/or Web of Science, and are
mostly journal papers written in English. Working papers, central-bank speeches 
and central-bank documents are therefore mostly absent from their analysis, 
so that their sample is very limited compared to ours: a few hundred documents 
compared to 4,710. In addition, they build their analysis with standard tools that mainly
describe what is studied, but not how (tone and stance) or why. Finally,
their classifications are never checked against a reading of the texts
themselves.

Our study addresses these three limits. Our corpus is much larger, with
papers coming from five databases, central-bank speeches and documents, in
different languages, and every text is checked to be written by an
economist or an economic institution. We ask not only what is written but
also who writes it, how, why and when. Finally, each automatic
classification we use is compared with our own reading of a sample of
texts.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}
\subsection{Sources and collection}

In order to properly use the tools and for better selection purposes, we try first to fetch as many documents as
possible. Indeed, if we had selected the sample based on pre-determined criteria of our choice, we could face
the bias of pre-selecting the sample. By having a larger sample with partly irrelevant data, we can apply selection
processes based on quality criteria, such as the relevance of a document. Also, it is useful to have a large sample
to be able to validate results on sub-samples or parts of the data kept for testing, which strengthen the results, or
for robustness tests.

When building the fetching algorithm, we therefore tried to reach for selection criteria as broad as possible. First,
we wanted many "types" of documents, namely: official academic papers published in journals or magazines, press articles,
speeches from institutional figures or official documents. The sources span :

\begin{itemize}
  \item Academic databases: OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, arXiv, HAL and DOAJ, which mainly contain working papers
  and journal articles, but also books.
  \item Institutional databases: speeches were mainly extracted from the BIS archive (as well as
  BIS RSS) and ECB's speech database. We also extracted from 26 central banks', the EU institutions and
  international institutions sites the pages relating to CBDC.
  \item Press: press articles are extracted from Google News
\end{itemize}

The searching process is fairly easy: we choose 115 sentences in 12 langues (using LLM and suggestions, as well
as our own formulations, based on common expressions in the literature), that are used to search accross titles and
abstracts alike. The APIs return results page by page for the papers and authorities' websites and
the press articles are harvested using a recursive bisection to single down the relevant articles. Everything
from all sources is collected and we can go over the filtering process.

\subsection{De-duplication and enrichment}

Because some articles may be duplicates or pre-publications or have similar names, we try to get rid
of all duplicates or fuse when they are from the same authors and relate to the same paper. The
contents of the documents are also enriched, in that we also harvest the language in which it is procuded,
the keywords of the text (using YAKE), the exact date (when possible, if not indexed at the beginning
of the year issued), the authors' names and media in which it has been issued, the introduction and
conclusion for the papers, which resume the motivations and results of the paper, as well as
the content of the text.


\subsection{Relevance: is CBDC the theme?}

Because CBDC is also a skin disease (Chronic Bullous Disease of Childhood) and a chemical term 
(CycloButane DiCarboxilate), or that it can be used as an acronym for anything that the author does not
explicit in the titles and abstracts, it is possible to obtain documents unrelated to the monetary concept.
Hence, using a range of contextual vocabulary (such as currency, payment, central bank, monnaie,
Geld, etc.), we filter for papers in which the term CBDC is ambiguous and not directly related to
Central Bank Digital Currency.

More importantly, we want CBDC to be the main theme of the paper, and not merely
a cited reference related to the actual content. Therefore, we establish relevance
scoring as such: each field $f \in F$ gets centrality score $c_f \in [0,1]$ (this term
will be also used later in the paper, but with a different meaning, be 
cautious !) that gives weight to the paper relevance if CBDC is a central
theme to the paper. The centrality scores are determined as such:

\begin{align*}
  c_{title} = \mathbf{1}[\text{CBDC in title}] \qquad 
  c_{kw} = 0.4 \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{CBDC keyword}] \\
  c_{abs} = \frac{\text{card}(\text{sentences mentioning CBDC})}{\text{card}(\text{sentences})}
\qquad c_{body} = 0.5 \min \left(1, \frac{\rho}{3} \right) + 0.5
\sigma
\end{align*}

Where $\rho = \text{mentions per 1000 words}$ and
$\sigma = \text{share of ten slices with a mention}$. And the relevance score is:

\begin{equation}
  \text{score}= 100 \cdot \left(0.6 \max_{f\in F}c_f + \frac{0.4}{\vert 
  F \vert} \sum_{f\in F} c_f\right)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Who counts as an economist}

We want now to ensure that the text has been written by an economist. This is
fairly easy since we can just check using the authors' ID for each academic
sources, which tells whether the author is an economist or not. Therefore,
all research document have been kept, when the relevance score clearly
indicated that the theme was CBDC. Moreover, since articles and speeches
issued by authorities are produced by either economists working there or 
economists by practice (C. Lagarde is a lawyer for example, but her vast 
experience as head of IMF and of ECB qualifies her as such). Press
articles and documents produces by governments were therefore dropped.


\subsection{Descriptive statistics}

The corpus analysed in this paper holds the 4{,}710 texts with CBDC as a 
theme that were written by economists (\cref{tab:descriptive}). Research 
makes up 85\% of it (3{,}994 texts); central bank speeches (511) and documents 
of central banks and international institutions (205) make up the rest. For 
three texts in four, CBDC is the main theme.

The first texts by 
economists date from 2016, and the field took off after 2019 (\cref{fig:years}). 
Research grew from 20 texts in 2017 to 839 in 2025, a compound rate of 
about 60\% a year, and 2026 had already exceeded 2025 by October. Speeches 
rose until 2022 (77) and have stayed close to 
70 a year since. Documents of institutions jumped in 2023 (49), the year of 
the European Commission's proposal for a digital euro and of the end of 
the ECB's investigation phase. The first period of the paper, 2014--2019 
rests on a small number of texts (211 texts, against 972 in 
2020--2022 and 3{,}360 in 2023--2026), which is 
why changes are estimated with intervals and tested against random dates 
(\cref{sec:how,sec:when}).

The growth rates above are compound annual rates: with $N_y$ texts in year $y$, the rate between $y_0$ and $y_1$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:growth}
g = \Big(\frac{N_{y_1}}{N_{y_0}}\Big)^{1/(y_1 - y_0)} - 1 ,
\end{equation}
so that research, with $N_{2017} = 20$ and $N_{2025} = 839$, grew by $g = (839/20)^{1/8} - 1 \approx 60\%$ a year.

Research is spread over 1{,}479 outlets, and working 
papers weigh as much as journals: the largest outlets are SSRN (461 texts)

\section{Who writes}\label{sec:who}
\subsection{Method: co-citation, co-authorship, bibliometric laws and distinctive vocabulary}

In the following analysis, to see who writes about CBDCs (which authors, institutions and papers), 
we use four different tools. The first one, called a co-citation network, links references that are 
cited in the same paper, which allows us to build groups of references often cited together. Then, 
we build a co-authorship network, which shows research teams by linking authors when they write a 
paper together. In both networks, to avoid giving too much weight to links between very popular references 
or authors, we weigh each link by its association strength, which takes into account how popular each item is. 

For two items $i$ and $j$ (references or authors), $c_{ij}$ is the number of papers in which they appear together and $c_i$ the number 
of papers in which $i$ appears. The weight of the link is the association strength given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:assoc}
s_{ij} = \frac{c_{ij}}{c_i\,c_j},
\end{equation}
proportional to the ratio of observed to expected co-occurrences (van Eck and Waltman, 2009). Groups are found with the Louvain algorithm
\parencite{blondel2008}, which maximises the modularity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modularity}
Q = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i,j}\Big[s_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m}\Big]\,\delta(g_i, g_j),
\end{equation}
where $k_i = \sum_j s_{ij}$, $m = \tfrac12\sum_{i,j} s_{ij}$, and $\delta(g_i, g_j) = 1$ when $i$ and $j$ are in the same group. This metric
effectively evaluates the quality of the network's division into "communities".


Besides these two networks, we also compute two standard bibliometric laws. The first one, Lotka’s law \parencite{lotka1926}, allows us to 
see how papers are spread among authors, whether a few authors write most of the papers or whether many 
authors only write one. Then, the second one, called Bradford’s law \parencite{bradford1934}, ranks publication sources by their number 
of papers on CBDCs and splits them into three groups of equal sizes in papers. This shows whether the literature 
is concentrated in a few sources or spread over many of them.

Lotka's law states that the share of authors with $n$ papers decreases as a power of $n$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lotka}
f(n) = \frac{C}{n^{\beta}},
\end{equation}
where $f(n)$ is the share of authors who write exactly $n$ papers on CBDCs, $C$ a normalising constant and 
$\beta$ the Lotka exponent, estimated by least squares on $\log f(n) = \log C - \beta \log n$; $\beta \approx 2$ in 
mature fields, and a larger $\beta$ means that more authors write a single paper. 
For Bradford's law, sources are sorted by decreasing number of papers and cut into 
three zones holding a third of the papers each, and the number of sources in each zone, 
$n_1 : n_2 : n_3$, measures concentration.



Finally, in order to compare the words used by research economists and central bankers, we use a method called 
log-odds ratio with an informative Dirichlet prior. Compared with simple word counts, this method does not give 
too much weight to rare words and allows us to see which words one group uses more than the other.

Let $y_{gw}$ be the number of times group $g$ uses word $w$, $n_g$ the total number of words of group $g$, and $\alpha_w = \alpha_0\,\pi_w$ a prior proportional to the frequency $\pi_w$ of $w$ in the whole corpus. For group $i$ against group $j$ (Monroe, Colaresi and Quinn, 2008),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logodds}
\delta_w = \log\frac{y_{iw}+\alpha_w}{n_i+\alpha_0-y_{iw}-\alpha_w} - \log\frac{y_{jw}+\alpha_w}{n_j+\alpha_0-y_{jw}-\alpha_w},
\qquad
z_w = \frac{\delta_w}{\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{y_{iw}+\alpha_w}+\dfrac{1}{y_{jw}+\alpha_w}}} ,
\end{equation}
and words are ranked by $z_w$. The prior pulls the log-odds of rare words towards zero, so that a word used twice by one group and never by the other does not come first.


\subsection{Findings}

From our analysis, the two 
largest sources are SSRN (461 papers) and RePEc (164 papers), two repositories of working papers. 
We also obtain a Lotka exponent of 3.3, which is higher than the value, of about 2, found in more 
mature fields: 83\% of authors write only one paper on CBDCs. In the same way, Bradford's law shows 
that only 21 sources publish a third of the papers, while the last third is spread over more than 
1,100 sources. In short, a small group of authors and sources publishes regularly on CBDCs, while 
most economists only write on the subject once.

Papers with at least one author from a central bank, the BIS, the IMF or the World Bank represent 10\% 
of the papers but receive 48\% of the citations, and their share of the papers fell from 23\% in 2014-2019 
to 7\% in 2023-2026. In other words, these economists write few papers but are cited almost five times more 
than their weight in the literature, while most new papers now come from economists outside these institutions. 
The co-citation network gives three groups of references, around Andolfatto (2020) and Brunnermeier (2019), 
Bordo (2017) and Barrdear (2016), and Auer (2022). However, these groups are not clearly separated since we 
have a low of $Q = 0.10$: the literature does not split into distinct schools of thought. In addition, the 
co-authorship network shows that research teams are small: about half of the papers are written by a single 
author, and only 15\% involve authors from different countries. Among institutions, the European Central Bank 
is by far the most active, with 118 speeches and 98 official documents. On the research side, Russia is the 
most represented country: it publishes the most papers, and four of the eight most active research institutions

\section{What is written}\label{sec:what}
\subsection{Method: thematic map, topic model and covariate-adjusted shares}

To see what is written by economists, we use two methods. The first one consists in 
grouping keywords that often appear together in the same papers into themes. Then, we 
place these themes on a thematic map with two axes: how much a theme is linked to the 
other themes (\emph{centrality}) and how much its keywords are linked to each other 
(\emph{density}). This allows us to classify themes into four types: motor, basic, niche, 
and emerging or declining. 

Formally, with $c_{ij}$ the number of papers where keywords $i$ and $j$ appear together and $c_i$ the number of papers with keyword $i$, the equivalence index is $e_{ij} = c_{ij}^2/(c_i c_j)$. For a theme $C$ (a group of keywords found as in \cref{eq:modularity}),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:callon}
\text{centrality}(C) = 10\!\!\sum_{i\in C,\; j\notin C}\!\! e_{ij},
\qquad
\text{density}(C) = \frac{100}{|C|}\sum_{i,j\in C} e_{ij},
\end{equation}
where $|C|$ is the number of keywords in $C$, and the four quadrants are separated by the medians of the two measures (Callon, Courtial and Laville, 1991; Cobo et al., 2011).


The second method consists in grouping texts by their meaning. To do so, we use \emph{SBERT}, 
a multilingual model that turns each title and abstract into a vector, so that two similar texts 
end up close, even if they are not written in the same language. We then group 
these vectors into 24 topics with \emph{BERTopic} and label each topic with its most specific words.

Each text $t$ is represented by a vector $\mathbf{e}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$, and the similarity of two 
texts $s$ and $t$ is the cosine
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cosine}
\cos(\mathbf{e}_s, \mathbf{e}_t) = \frac{\mathbf{e}_s\cdot\mathbf{e}_t}{\lVert\mathbf{e}_s\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{e}_t\rVert}.
\end{equation}
The vectors are reduced to five dimensions with UMAP and grouped with HDBSCAN, which finds dense regions 
without fixing the number of topics in advance. Each topic $c$ is then described by the 
words with the highest class-based TF-IDF,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ctfidf}
W_{w,c} = \frac{\text{tf}_{w,c}}{|c|}\,\log\Big(1 + \frac{A}{f_w}\Big),
\end{equation}
where $\text{tf}_{w,c}$ is the number of occurrences of word $w$ in topic $c$ (all texts of the topic put together), 
$|c|$ the total number of words in topic $c$, $f_w$ the number of occurrences of $w$ in all topics and $A$ the average number of words per topic 
(Grootendorst, 2022).


Finally, to make sure that a change in the share of a topic is not only due to a change in who 
writes, we run a logit regression of each topic on the period, the type of author and the language. 
That allows us to measure how the share of each topic changed between two periods (here, between 
2014-2019 and 2023-2026), in percentage points, all else being equal.

For text $t$ and an outcome $y_t \in \{0,1\}$ (here, belonging to a topic),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logit}
P(y_t = 1) = \Lambda\big(\alpha + \beta_{\text{period}(t)} + \gamma_{\text{actor}(t)} + \theta\,\mathbf{1}[\text{English}_t]\big),
\qquad \Lambda(x) = \frac{1}{1+\exp(-x)},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the intercept; $\beta_p$ is the effect of period $p \in \{\text{2020--2022}, \text{2023--2026}\}$, with 
2014--2019 as the reference ($\beta_{\text{2014--2019}} = 0$); $\gamma_{\text{actor}(t)}$ is the effect of the type of author of $t$, 
equal to $0$ for research economists and to $\gamma$ for central banks and institutions; $\mathbf{1}[\text{English}_t]$ equals $1$ 
when $t$ is written in English and $0$ otherwise, and $\theta$ is its effect; $\Lambda$ is the logistic function. The model is 
estimated by maximum likelihood with robust (HC1) standard errors. The change reported is the average marginal effect of the period,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ame}
\Delta = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big[\hat P\big(y_t = 1 \mid \text{2023--2026}, x_t\big) - \hat P\big(y_t = 1 \mid \text{2014--2019}, x_t\big)\Big],
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the number of texts, $\hat P(y_t = 1 \mid p, x_t)$ is the probability predicted by \cref{eq:logit} for text $t$ when its 
period is set to $p$, and $x_t$ holds the actor and the language of text $t$, kept at their observed values; its 95\% confidence interval is obtained by drawing the coefficients 400 times from their estimated distribution. This is the prevalence part of a structural topic model (Roberts et al., 2014), applied to the topics found by BERTopic.



\subsection{Findings}

The thematic map shows that the CBDC literature is driven by two themes: blockchain technology 
and the digital transformation of financial services, together with monetary policy and payments. 
Fintech and digital finance form a basic theme, while digital payments, India and the cashless 
society are niche topics.

that lost the most ground since then ($-13.2$ points).

\section{How economists discuss CBDCs: tone and stance}\label{sec:how}
\subsection{Method: tone (FinBERT) and stance (reading and a local language model)}
We analyze both tone and stance, as they capture two different aspects of the discussion around CBDCs. 
Tone measures whether the language used to discuss CBDCs is positive or negative, whereas stance captures 
the author’s overall position toward CBDCs for instance whether the text is favorable, neutral, or critical.
For the tone analysis, we keep the English sentences that explicitly mention a CBDC and apply \textit{FinBERT}, 
a model trained on financial text. For each sentence, the model gives three probabilities: \(P(+)\), \(P(0)\) and \(P(-)\) . 
We define the tone of sentence $s$ and of document $d$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tone}
\text{Tone}_s = P_s(+) - P_s(-) \in [-1, 1],
\qquad
\text{Tone}_d = \frac{1}{S_d}\sum_{s \in d} \text{Tone}_s ,
\end{equation}
where $P_s(+)$ and $P_s(-)$ are the probabilities that sentence $s$ is positive and negative, and $S_d$ is the number of 
CBDC sentences in document $d$. A tone of $1$ means certainly positive, $-1$ certainly negative and $0$ neutral or balanced.
The scores are first averaged within each document and then across groups and years. This avoids giving too much 
weight to longer texts. In order to check the quality of the measure, 90 sentences from economists’ texts were also 
coded by hand. The agreement between FinBERT and manual coding gives a Cohen’s \(\kappa\) of 0.52.

For stance, we use two approaches. First, texts are read manually to estimate the level of favourable, neutral 
and critical positions. The share of favourable texts is reported with a Wilson confidence interval:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wilson}
\frac{
\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n}
\pm
z \sqrt{
\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}
+
\frac{z^2}{4n^2}
}
}{
1+\frac{z^2}{n}
},
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of texts read in the group, $\hat p = k/n$ is the share of them, $k$, judged favourable, and 
$z = 1.96$ is the standard normal quantile for a 95\% interval. Unlike the usual interval $\hat p \pm z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$, 
it stays within $[0, 1]$ and remains reliable for small samples and shares close to 0 or 1.

Manual reading gives a more reliable measure of the level of support, however it cannot easily be applied to 
the whole corpus. To study changes over time, we therefore use a local \textit{Qwen2.5 7B} model on random 
samples of texts for each actor and period. The change in stance is measured as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dstance}
\Delta \hat{p} = \hat{p}_{\text{late}} - \hat{p}_{\text{early}},
\end{equation}
where $\hat p_{\text{early}}$ and $\hat p_{\text{late}}$ are the shares of texts labelled favourable by the model among the 
$n_{\text{early}}$ texts of 2014--2019 and the $n_{\text{late}}$ texts of 2023--2026 of the sample (up to 100 per actor and period), 
with an approximate 95\% confidence interval given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dstance-ci}
\Delta \hat{p}
\pm
1.96
\sqrt{
\frac{\hat{p}_{\text{late}}\left(1-\hat{p}_{\text{late}}\right)}{n_{\text{late}}}
+
\frac{\hat{p}_{\text{early}}\left(1-\hat{p}_{\text{early}}\right)}{n_{\text{early}}}
}.
\end{equation}
The local LLM reaches a Cohen’s \(\kappa\) of 0.45 against manual coding, but it tends to classify too many texts as favourable. 
For that reason, we use manual reading for the level of stance, and the LLM mainly to compare changes across periods

\subsection{Findings}
The analysis reveals a clear increase in the positivity of CBDC-related discourse over time. Among research economists, average 
net tone rises from approximately 0.07 in 2019 to 0.25 in 2025, while central-bank and institutional texts remain slightly more 
positive throughout the period. Overall, the average net tone is 0.19 for research economists and 0.22 for central banks and 
institutions. This suggests that the language used to discuss CBDCs has become progressively more positive in both groups, 
although institutional discourse is consistently somewhat more favourable in tone.

However, the stance analysis shows a stronger difference between the two groups. In the manually coded sample, research economists 
are predominantly neutral: only 8\% of texts are classified as favourable and 2\% as critical. By contrast, 52\% of central-bank 
and institutional texts are favourable, while 6\% are critical. This indicates a difference in the role played by each group in 
the CBDC debate: academic research is mainly analytical, whereas central banks and institutions are more likely to express explicit 
support for CBDCs.

The evolution over time points in the same direction. The local language model reveals that the share of favourable research texts 
rises from 26\% in 2014–2019 to 40\% in 2023–2026, corresponding to an increase of 14 percentage points. The increase is substantially 
larger for central banks and institutions, from 25\% to 65\%, or 40 percentage points. Since the LLM tends to overestimate the absolute 
level of favourable stance, these percentages should not be interpreted as precise levels of support, rather, they indicate the direction 
and magnitude of change across periods.

Overall, the results point to a gradual change in the way CBDCs are discussed. The language becomes more positive in both groups, 
but the shift towards an explicitly favourable stance is much stronger among central banks and institutions. Research economists 
remain more cautious and analytical, while institutional actors increasingly express support for CBDCs as the debate develops.

\subsection{Do policy events move the tone?}
To assess whether major policy developments are associated with sudden changes in CBDC discourse, we compare the average tone of texts 
published in the 180 days before and after each of twelve major CBDC-related events. The events include, for instance, the Facebook’s 
announcement of Libra, the ECB’s digital euro report, the launch of the Sand Dollar and eNaira, and the EU digital euro proposal. 
For each event, the change in tone is defined as the difference between the post-event and pre-event averages.

To determine whether the observed changes are unusually large, we compare them with changes calculated around 500 randomly selected dates. 
This gives us a simple comparison point: if the change around a real event is not larger than what we often observe around random dates, 
it is difficult to argue that the event itself produced a clear shift in tone.

The results provide no evidence of a discrete change in tone around any of the twelve events. None of the estimated changes is 
statistically significant, and the smallest \(p\)-value equal to 0.24. For instance, the announcement of Libra is associated 
with a decline in tone of  \(-0.04\), while the launch of the eNaira is followed by an increase of \(+0.05\), yet neither 
change is unusual relative to the placebo distribution.

These findings suggest that the increasingly positive tone documented in the previous section is gradual rather than driven 
by one specific policy announcement or launch. Instead, the change seems to be gradual. Thus, the evolution of CBDC discourse 
appears to move alongside the broader policy environment. Policy developments may still matter, but their effect is more likely 
to build up over time rather than appear as an immediate jump in tone.

\section{Why: stated motivations}\label{sec:why}
\subsection{Method: a validated codebook}
To identify the main motivations used for study and develop CBDCs, we construct a rule-based codebook covering ten motivations. 
The objective is to capture motivations that are explicitly stated by the authors, rather than simply detecting whether a topic 
appears somewhere in the text.

The codebook was built from a development set of 100 introductions. For each motivation, we  identified the expressions authors 
use to state it and translated these expressions into regular-expression in seven languages. The resulting rules include, 
for example, expressions related to \textit{financial inclusion}, such as “\textit{unbanked}” or “\textit{access to financial services}”, 
and to \textit{monetary sovereignty}, such as “\textit{global stablecoin}”, “\textit{Libra}”, or “\textit{dollarization}”. 
The final codebook was then applied to 4,110 texts. 

The codebook was then evaluated on a separate test set of 74 texts, which was not used during its construction. These texts were coded 
manually, and performance was assessed on the 50 documents written by economists. We use three main validation measures, computed on the $50 \times 10 = 500$ (text, motivation) pairs: for each pair, 
the codebook and the reader each say whether the text states the motivation. A true positive ($TP$) is a pair where both 
say yes, a false positive ($FP$) a pair where only the codebook says yes, and a false negative ($FN$) a pair where only 
the reader says yes. Precision captures the share of detected motivations that are correctly identified:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:precision}
\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP},
\end{equation}
while recall measures how many of the motivations identified by human reading are recovered by the codebook:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recall}
\text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}.
\end{equation}
Finally, Cohen’s \(\kappa\) measures agreement between the codebook and manual coding beyond what would be expected by chance:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kappa}
\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e},
\qquad
p_e = q_r\, q_c + (1 - q_r)(1 - q_c),
\end{equation}
where \(p_o\) is the observed agreement, the share of pairs on which the codebook and the reader give the same answer, and 
\(p_e\) the agreement expected by chance, with \(q_r\) and \(q_c\) the shares of pairs coded yes by the reader and by the codebook. 
$\kappa = 1$ means perfect agreement and $\kappa = 0$ no more agreement than chance.

On the held-out economists’ sample, the codebook reaches a precision of 0.70, a recall of 0.41, and a Cohen’s \(\kappa\) of 0.46. 
We also compare its performance with several machine-based alternatives, including \textit{SBERT classifiers}, an \textit{NLI model} 
and local \textit{Qwen2.5} language models, using the same validation texts.

\subsection{Findings}
The results show that the reasons given for discussing CBDCs have changed over time. In the first period, monetary policy was one 
of the main motivations, appearing in 36.8\% of texts in 2014–2019. This share falls to 24.4\% in 2023–2026, which corresponds 
to an adjusted decrease of 15.1 percentage points. The decline of cash also becomes less important over time, with an estimated decrease of 6.5 points.

By contrast, several motivations become more prominent. Financial inclusion rises from 3.8\% of texts in 2014–2019 to 16.8\% 
in 2023–2026, corresponding to an adjusted increase of 12.3 points.  Geopolitics and sanctions show a similarly strong increase 
of 12.4 points, while cross-border payments increase by 8.7 points. In addition to that, payment efficiency and privacy and surveillance 
become more frequent motivations. 

Overall, these results suggest that the way CBDCs are justified has gradually changed. Earlier discussions were more focused on monetary 
policy and the decline of cash, while recent texts place more emphasis on financial inclusion, payments and geopolitical issues.

There are also some differences between research economists and central banks. Central-bank and institutional texts are less likely 
than research papers to frame CBDCs through monetary policy, technology, financial inclusion or cross-border payments, while the difference 
for geopolitics and sanctions is close to zero.

Given the relatively low recall of the codebook, these percentages should not be read as exact measures of prevalence. The main interest 
lies in the direction and relative magnitude of the changes across periods rather than in the absolute levels themselves.

\section{When: the policy environment}\label{sec:when}
\subsection{Method: bursts, event study with placebo dates, difference-in-differences}
To examine how the CBDC discussion evolves alongside its policy environment, we use three complementary methods.
First, we use \textit{Kleinberg's burst detection algorithm} to identify periods in which some keywords become much more frequent. 
For keyword \(w\) in quarter \(t\), \(r_t\) is the number of papers using the keyword among the \(d_t\) research papers of that quarter. The model compares a normal 
state, with rate \(p_0 = R/D\), where \(R = \sum_t r_t\) and \(D = \sum_t d_t\) are the totals over all quarters, to a burst state, with a higher rate \(p_1 = 2p_0\). 
The cost of being in state \(i \in \{0, 1\}\) in quarter \(t\) is minus the log-likelihood of the observed count,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:burst}
\sigma_i(t) = -\ln\Big[\binom{d_t}{r_t}\, p_i^{\,r_t}\,(1 - p_i)^{\,d_t - r_t}\Big],
\end{equation}
and moving from the normal to the burst state costs an extra \(\ln Q\), where \(Q\) is the number of quarters, while leaving a burst is free. Since entering a burst has a cost, short random 
fluctuations are less likely to be selected. The final sequence of normal and burst periods, the one with the lowest total cost, is chosen using the \textit{Viterbi algorithm}.

Second, we run an event study with placebo dates to see whether attention to CBDCs increases immediately after major policy events. 
We estimate a baseline publication rate over the period from 90 to 15 days before each event, 
\(\lambda = N_{\text{pre}}/76\), where \(N_{\text{pre}}\) is the number of texts dated to the day published in these 76 days. The expected number of texts in 
the 15 days starting on the day of the event is then:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expected}
E = 15\lambda
\end{equation}
and the observed increase is measured using:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zevent}
z = \frac{N-E}{\sqrt{E}},
\end{equation}
where \(N\) is the number of texts actually published in these 15 days. If publications followed a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\), 
\(z\) would be approximately standard normal.
Because publication activity is not stable over time, each event is compared with 500 random placebo dates. 
The \(p\)-value is the share of placebo dates with a larger \(z\)-score than the actual event.

Finally, we use a difference-in-differences approach to examine whether geopolitical motivations became more important after 2021, 
especially in texts related to Russia. The outcome variable indicates whether a motivation is stated, while the treatment group 
includes texts about Russia or written in Russian:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:did}
y_{it}
=
\alpha + \eta\, T_i + \mu_t
+
\sum_{k \neq 2021}
\gamma_k T_i \mathbf{1}[t = k]
+
\varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where \(y_{it}\) equals 1 if text \(i\), published in year \(t\) (2018--2026), states the motivation and 0 otherwise; \(T_i\) equals 1 
if text \(i\) is in the treatment group and 0 otherwise; \(\alpha\) is the intercept, \(\eta\) the permanent gap between the two groups, 
\(\mu_t\) a year effect common to all texts (\(\mu_{2021} = 0\)), \(\mathbf{1}[t = k]\) equals 1 if \(t = k\), and \(\varepsilon_{it}\) is the error term. 
The model is a linear probability model estimated by ordinary least squares with robust (HC1) standard errors. 
The coefficients \(\gamma_k\) measure how the gap between Russia-related texts and the rest of the corpus changes in each year relative to 2021. 
This allows us to see whether the post-2021 shift is stronger in Russia-related discourse than in the broader CBDC literature. 
The coefficients for the years before 2021 test whether the two groups followed parallel trends before the shock. The single effect 
reported in the findings comes from the same model in which the yearly interactions are replaced by one term, 
\(\gamma\, T_i \mathbf{1}[t > 2021]\).

\subsection{Findings}
The results show that the focus of the CBDC debate changes quite clearly over time. 
In the earlier years, the main bursts are related to monetary policy, economic models 
and economic impact. More recent bursts are instead linked to India, monetary sovereignty 
and the digital euro. This suggests a shift from a more theoretical discussion toward 
topics that are more closely connected to specific countries and policy developments.  

Besides, policy events also seem to matter for the amount of attention given to CBDCs, 
although not in every case. Out of the twelve events considered, four are followed 
by a rise in publication activity that is larger than 95\% of the placebo dates. 
The strongest increases are observed around the ECB preparation phase and the launch 
of the Sand Dollar. At the same time, several other major events do not generate a noticeable
 jump in attention. 

The results on Russia give a similar picture. After 2021, texts related to Russia became more likely 
to mention geopolitics and sanctions as a motivation for CBDCs. The increase is about 11.6 percentage 
points larger than in the rest of the corpus, and the gap becomes wider over time. 

Overall, the results suggest that the CBDC discussion evolves together with changes in the policy 
and geopolitical environment. Some events create short-term peaks in attention, while broader 
geopolitical developments seem to be associated with changes in the way CBDCs are discussed. 
However, these results should not be read as direct proof of causality

\section{The discussion and its environment}\label{sec:environment}

In this section, we put together the previous results period by period, and we discuss 
what they allow us to say, or not, about the causes of the changes we observe. 

\subsection{Period by period}

From our analysis, we observe that between 2014 and 2019, economists wrote little about CBDCs, 
with 53 texts a year on average. The discussion was mainly about monetary policy: it is the most 
frequent motivation, and keywords such as monetary policy and economic theories suddenly become 
much more frequent during this period. The main event of this period, the announcement of Facebook’s 
project Libra in June 2019, is not followed by any rise in attention. In addition, the tone of the 
texts remains cautious: the net tone is only 0.06 for research economists and 0.10 for central banks. 

Between 2020 and 2022, the number of texts increases to 324 a year. This is the period corresponding 
to the first central bank reports and launches, but only two of the seven events of the period are 
followed by a significant rise in attention: the ECB report on a digital euro and the launch of the 
Sand Dollar in the Bahamas, both in October 2020. At the same time, the discussion becomes more 
favorable, especially among central banks, whose net tone rises to 0.22. 

Finally, since 2023, economists write about 840 texts a year, and the discussion is now about national 
projects. Out of the four events of this period, only two lead to more texts: the start of the ECB 
preparation phase and, just barely, the EU bill on the digital euro. Keywords such as India, its Unified 
Payments Interface and monetary sovereignty also become much more frequent. This is also the period when 
texts are the most positive about CBDCs: compared with 2014-2019, the share of texts in favor of CBDCs 
rose by 40 points for central banks and by 14 points for research economists. In short, economists moved 
from a theoretical question on monetary policy to real national projects and became more positive as these 
projects moved forward.


\subsection{What can and cannot be said about causes}

Our analysis is purely descriptive: it allows us to see what changes and what moves at the same time, but 
not to prove what causes these changes. To see how far we can go, we test three links. The first one is 
between events and attention: 4 of the 12 policy events are followed by more texts than 95\% of random 
dates. In other words, economists write more after some events, but this only tells us about timing and 
not about causes. Then, by testing the link between events and tone, we observe that none of the 12 events 
changes the tone of the texts: the tone rises slowly over the whole period, without any sudden change after 
a specific event. Finally, we observe that after the sanctions on Russia in 2022, the share of texts about 
Russia, or written in Russian, that mention geopolitics and sanctions rose by 11.6 points more than in other 
texts. However, three other motivations also changed in these texts (financial inclusion, financial stability 
and monetary policy), so this result fits with an effect of the sanctions but does not prove it.

\section{Robustness}\label{sec:robustness}

In order to test whether our analysis really shows the economists' debate and is not 
driven by the way we built the data, we test the robustness of our main results. 

To build our corpus and our measures, we made several choices that could affect the results. 
For example, the fall of monetary policy could simply come from the growing share of texts 
written in Russian, which may discuss CBDCs differently, and not from a real change in the debate. 
Therefore, to check for robustness, we run the same calculations on six different samples in which 
we change one choice at a time: 

\begin{itemize} 
\item only texts where CBDC is the main theme.
 \item only texts in English, since the mix of languages changes over time.
 \item only journal articles for research, since working papers may differ from published work.
\item only texts before 2026.
\item a less strict coding of motivations, since our codebook misses many of them.
\item research from every discipline, to check that our filter on economists does not create the results by itself. 
\end{itemize}

\input{tables/tab_robustness}

The results we obtained seem to be very stable. Across all samples, the rise of financial inclusion 
lies between $+11.8$ and $+15.6$ points and the fall of monetary policy between $-8.8$ and $-16.8$ points, 
and both changes are statistically significant in every sample. With English texts only, the fall of monetary 
policy is almost unchanged ($-16.0$ points), so it does not come from the growing share of Russian texts. It 
is smaller with the less strict coding of motivations, but still significant. Central banks are also more 
favourable to CBDCs than research economists in every sample, by 11 to 16 points, the gap in tone is always 
small (0.01 to 0.03), and the same four events are followed by a rise in attention each time. In short, our 
main results do not depend on the way we built the corpus. However, these tests only tell us that they are 
stable, not what causes them.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

From our analysis, we observe that economists increasingly see CBDCs less as a monetary policy instrument and 
more as a tool for inclusion, payments and geopolitics. Monetary policy, central in the early literature lost 
ground, while financial stability remained a constant concern. Monetary sovereignty also stays stable, while 
geopolitics and sanctions are one of the motivations that grow the most. Indeed, the international dimension of 
CBDCs, through geopolitics and cross-border payments, has become much more important.

Central banks also play a key role in this debate. Economists from central banks and international institutions 
write 10\% of the research papers but receive 48\% of the citations, and central bank texts are much more 
favorable to CBDCs than those of research economists, who remain mostly neutral. This means that the most cited 
work on CBDCs comes largely from institutions that are directly involved in the policy debate, and not only from 
independent research.


\subsection{Limitations}

However, our study also highlights some limitations. First, we identify economists by the field of their texts 
and the institution of their authors, and not by their education. Some economists who publish in other fields 
may therefore be missed, while some non-economists may be kept. Then, our measures of tone only cover part of 
the debate since it is measured on English texts only, which represent 79\% of the corpus. Therefore, it mainly 
reflects the English-speaking debate, while the share of texts in other languages, like Russian ones, grows over 
time. Finally, our analysis is descriptive: it shows how the debate changes, but not why. For example, explaining 
why central banks became more favorable to CBDCs would require other data, such as interviews with economists or 
a study of their careers.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

In this paper, we studied how economists discuss CBDCs, using 4,710 texts written by economists between 2014 
and 2026, coming from research databases and central banks. By combining bibliometric methods with text analysis, 
we tried to answer five questions: what is written, who writes it, why, how and when. 

From our analysis, we observe that the debate grew very quickly, by about 60\% per year between 2017 and 2025, 
but remains concentrated. Most authors write only one paper on CBDCs, while a few institutions publish the most. 
Over time, the debate also became more national, first around China and Russia, with the e-CNY and the digital 
ruble, and more recently around India and digital euro. In addition, texts on CBDCs became more positive over 
time. We also observe that economists reacted mostly to European steps, such as the European legislative proposal,
 while other events, like the announcement of Libra or the launches of CBDCs in Nigeria and India, were not 
 followed by a rise in attention. This may partly reflect the weight of the ECB, which is the most active 
 institution in our corpus. Finally, our main results on motivations, stance and events remain the same across 
 all our robustness tests. 

Future work could apply the same approach to other disciplines, such as law or computer science. It could also 
study how economists discuss stablecoins, which appear as an emerging theme in our data and may become the main 
alternative to CBDCs in future discussions.

\section{Search vocabulary}\label{app:vocabulary}

A single vocabulary drives both the searches and the relevance score (\cref{sec:data}). It has 31 concepts, each written as a case-insensitive regular expression, and 115 search phrases derived from them, in 12 languages. Three principles guided it.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Recall first.} The vocabulary covers the generic term in every major language, the names of national projects, the BIS Innovation Hub experiments, and the older names used before ``CBDC'' became standard (\emph{digital fiat currency}, \emph{central bank cryptocurrency}, \emph{Fedcoin}, \emph{RSCoin}, \emph{CADcoin}, \emph{digital base money}).
\item \textbf{Strong and ambiguous terms.} A \emph{strong} term identifies a central bank digital currency on its own (\emph{central bank digital currency}, \emph{digital euro}, \emph{e-CNY}). An \emph{ambiguous} term does not: ``CBDC'' is also \emph{chronic bullous dermatosis of childhood}, ``DCEP'' a chemical compound, ``Project Stella'' a satellite. Ambiguous terms count only when the document also contains monetary context: \emph{currency, monetary, payment, central bank, banking, ledger, stablecoin}, and their equivalents in French, Spanish, Portuguese, German, Italian, Russian, Chinese, Japanese and Korean. When the only evidence is in the body of the text, a stricter context list is required (\emph{central bank, digital currency, monetary, payments}).
\item \textbf{Never-scoring terms.} \emph{Digital dollar} and \emph{Sand Dollar} are reported but never scored: they are too often used for private tokens or unrelated products.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Harvesting algorithms}\label{app:harvest}

\subsection{Sources}
Records come from four families of sources (\cref{tab:sources}). Nothing is filtered at harvest time: every record found by a search is kept, and relevance is decided afterwards on the merged corpus. Every step writes a checkpoint, so an interrupted harvest resumes where it stopped.

\begin{description}
\item[Academic databases.] OpenAlex (journal articles, working papers, books, chapters, preprints, reports), Semantic Scholar, arXiv, HAL and DOAJ. Each search phrase is queried in titles and abstracts; results are merged on the source's identifier. OpenAlex reference lists are resolved into citation strings, which the co-citation analysis uses.
\item[Central bankers' speeches.] The BIS bulk archive (all central banks, 1996 onwards, full text), the ECB's speech dataset (full text, including non-English originals), and the BIS RSS feed for speeches published after the bulk archive.
\item[Authorities.] A crawl starting from 26 CBDC pages of central banks, EU institutions and international organisations, following links one level deep within the same site, PDFs included.
\item[Press.] Google News, searched through its RSS interface in 55 combinations of country edition, language and query. GDELT was also queried but throttled the connection for hours after a few requests, so it contributed nothing to the final corpus.
\end{description}

\input{tables/sources}

\subsection{Pagination and rate limits}
Academic APIs return results page by page; the harvester follows the API's cursor (200 records per page for OpenAlex) until no page is left. Requests are spaced by a minimum interval $\Delta$,
\[ t_{k+1} - t_k \ge \Delta, \]
with $\Delta = 0.12$\,s for OpenAlex, 3.5\,s for arXiv and 1.2\,s for Google News. After the $j$-th consecutive refusal (HTTP 429 or 5xx), the harvester waits $w_j = \min(60j, 900)$ seconds before retrying.

\subsection{Press: recursive bisection of time windows}
A Google News search returns at most about 100 items, whereas a single month can hold thousands of articles. Each search is therefore run on time windows that are split until every window falls below the cap:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Start from monthly windows $W = [a, b)$ covering January 2000 to the harvest date, on a stack.
\item Pop a window and fetch the items published in it.
\item If it returns $N(W) \ge 95$ items and lasts more than one day, split it at $m = a + (b-a)/2$, push $[m, b)$ and $[a, m)$, and go back to step 2.
\item Otherwise save the items and checkpoint the window.
\end{enumerate}
A month is split at most $\lceil \log_2 31 \rceil = 5$ times, and keeping $L$ windows costs $2L - 1$ requests. Google News limits requests over time, and the harvest was stopped with about 15 of the 55 searches unfinished; press counts are therefore incomplete, and skewed towards recent years.

\subsection{Full text, enrichment and de-duplication}
\begin{description}
\item[Full text.] PDFs are extracted with PyMuPDF and web pages with trafilatura, which also recovers the page's date, author and outlet when the record lacks them. Texts are split into introduction, conclusion and section headings; for speeches and press, the opening and closing paragraphs play these roles.
\item[Enrichment.] Language is detected with \texttt{langdetect}; keywords are extracted with YAKE (one to three words) when a record has no author or indexed keywords. Dates are kept with their precision (day, month, year), and 1 January dates are flagged, since they often stand for a year only.
\item[De-duplication.] Records are matched on a key: the DOI for academic records, else their normalised title and year; the date and normalised title for speeches; the URL for other records. A second pass merges academic records with the same normalised title and year but different DOIs (a preprint and its published version, for instance). Merged records keep the most precise date, the longest abstract and the union of keywords and sources.
\end{description}

\subsection{Relevance score}
Each available field $f$ of a record receives a centrality $c_f\in[0,1]$: 1 for a title containing a strong term; 0.4 for a keyword list containing one (keywords are partly machine-assigned); for the abstract, the share of its sentences that contain one; for the body, half the density of mentions (per 1{,}000 words, saturating at 3) and half their spread (the share of ten equal slices of the text that contain one). The score is
\[ \text{score} = 100\Big(0.6\,\max_{f\in F} c_f + 0.4\,\frac{1}{|F|}\sum_{f\in F} c_f\Big), \]
where $F$ is the set of fields available, so a missing abstract or full text is not penalised. Records scoring at least 50 are labelled \emph{main theme}, from 25 to 49 \emph{secondary theme}, and below 25 \emph{mentioned only}. Records whose only matches are ambiguous terms without monetary context are \emph{ambiguous terms only}; records without any match are \emph{off-topic}; press items whose text could not be retrieved are \emph{unverified}. Records with CBDC as a theme but dated before 2014 were all homonyms or misdated, and are labelled \emph{dated before 2014}. \Cref{tab:relevance} gives the labels and their validation.

The samples were drawn at random; the motivation, stance and tone samples were topped up with texts written by economists so that each holds at least 50 of them (\cref{tab:samples}). Coding was done blind to the machine labels, by one coder assisted by a language model.

\subsection{Coding guide}
\begin{description}
\item[Relevance.] Is a central bank digital currency the theme of the text, or one of its main themes? A passing mention, a homonym, or a text about cryptocurrencies or stablecoins only is coded \emph{no}.
\item[Motivations.] Unit: the first 1{,}500 characters of the introduction, else of the abstract, else of the text. A motivation is coded when the text raises it as a reason to study, issue or worry about a CBDC; a text can have several. The ten motivations are defined as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Decline of cash}: cash use is falling, or access to cash or public money is at risk.
\item \emph{Financial inclusion}: reaching unbanked or underbanked people; access to financial services.
\item \emph{Monetary sovereignty}: a threat from private money, stablecoins, Big Tech, cryptocurrencies or foreign currencies to the central bank's control of money.
\item \emph{Payment efficiency}: cheaper, faster, more efficient, resilient or innovative domestic payments.
\item \emph{Cross-border payments}: international payments, remittances, cross-border settlement.
\item \emph{Monetary policy}: monetary policy, interest rates, transmission, money supply.
\item \emph{Financial stability}: bank disintermediation, bank runs, deposit outflows, risks to financial stability.
\item \emph{Privacy and surveillance}: privacy, anonymity, data protection, surveillance or control of users.
\item \emph{Geopolitics and sanctions}: geopolitics, sanctions, dollar dominance, internationalisation of a currency.
\item \emph{Technology}: blockchain, distributed ledgers, tokenisation, smart contracts, programmable money.
\end{itemize}
\item[Stance.] Unit: the title and abstract (research), or the title and the first five sentences that name a CBDC (speeches, documents). \emph{Favourable}: supports CBDCs or presents them positively; \emph{critical}: opposes them or stresses their risks and problems; \emph{neutral}: descriptive or balanced. Analysing risks is neutral; arguing that they can be solved so that a CBDC can be issued, or recommending issuance, is favourable.
\item[Tone.] Unit: one English sentence that names a CBDC. \emph{Positive} or \emph{negative} when the sentence presents CBDCs or their effects favourably or unfavourably; \emph{neutral} when it describes, defines or reports.
\end{description}

\section{Data and code availability}\label{app:data}

\subsection{Data}
The corpus is a single file, \texttt{data/cbdc\_corpus.csv}: one row per document and 49 columns, in UTF-8. Every record keeps its issuer, its date with the precision of that date, its original language, its keywords (author, indexed and extracted), its abstract, introduction and conclusion when available, its full text, its relevance label and theme score, and the sources it was found in. The harvest ran from 8 to 11 October 2026; it covers 48{,}188 documents, of which 17{,}709 have CBDC as a theme and 4{,}710 were written by economists (\cref{app:harvest}). The build step can also export it in two formats read by bibliometric software: a Scopus-style CSV (for \texttt{bibliometrix} and VOSviewer) and RIS.

Caches hold the results that are slow to recompute: sentence embeddings, sentence tones, the language model's stance labels, and the OpenAlex fields and institutions of each paper. The hand-coded validation samples and every coder's predictions are in \texttt{presentation/} (\cref{tab:samples}).

Many full texts are under copyright. The data are therefore shared with the members of the project and on request, not published openly. The metadata, identifiers (DOI, OpenAlex and source identifiers, URLs) and the code that rebuilds the corpus from the public sources are not restricted.

\subsection{Code}
All code is in Python and kept in a version-controlled repository, available from the authors on request. One command per step rebuilds the corpus, and every step resumes where it stopped:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \texttt{run.py harvest}: query every source (\cref{app:harvest});
\item \texttt{run.py fulltext}: download and extract the full texts;
\item \texttt{run.py build}: merge, de-duplicate, enrich and score relevance;
\item \texttt{scripts/tag\_economists.py}: retrieve the OpenAlex field and institutions of each paper;
\item \texttt{scripts/validate\_coders.py}: score every candidate coder against the hand-coded samples;
\item \texttt{scripts/make\_appendix.py}: write the tables of this appendix (\texttt{paper/tables/}).
\end{enumerate}
The analyses (networks, topics, motivations, tone, stance, event studies, robustness) are in \texttt{cbdc\_biblio/analysis.py} and \texttt{cbdc\_biblio/nlp.py}.

\subsection{Software and models}
Python 3.10 or later; the packages and their versions are listed in \texttt{requirements.txt}. The models are open and run locally:
\begin{itemize}
\item sentence embeddings: \texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2} (Sentence-Transformers);
\item tone: FinBERT (\texttt{ProsusAI/finbert});
\item natural language inference (tested coder): \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3-base-xnli-multilingual-nli-2mil7};
\item stance: Qwen2.5 7B Instruct, 4-bit, run with \texttt{mlx-lm} (Apple Silicon); Qwen2.5 3B was tested.
\end{itemize}
Everything ran on a laptop (Apple M1, 8\,GB of memory). Coding new texts with the language model requires Apple Silicon; every other step runs on any machine once the caches are present.

\section{Use of artificial intelligence and statement of originality}\label{app:ai}

\subsection{Use of artificial intelligence}
Artificial intelligence was used in two distinct ways in this work.

\paragraph{As research instruments.} Several language models are part of the method and are described with it: multilingual sentence embeddings (topics), FinBERT (tone), a natural language inference model and two local Qwen2.5 models (tested as coders; the 7B model codes stance). Each of them was evaluated against a reading of the texts before being used, and their scores are reported in \cref{app:validation}. They run locally; no text of the corpus was sent to an external service for coding.

\paragraph{As assistants.} A general-purpose AI assistant helped to write and debug the code that collects, cleans and analyses the corpus, to produce tables and figures, and to draft parts of the technical description in the appendices. The hand coding of the validation samples was AI-assisted: texts were read and coded blind to the machine labels, with the help of the assistant. For this reason, the validation scores measure agreement with an AI-assisted reading.

The research question, the choice and interpretation of the methods, the reading of the results and the conclusions are the authors'. Every number reported in the paper is computed by the code from the data, and the authors checked the text, the code and the results; they take full responsibility for the content of the paper.
